The lecture itself

University lecture courses

The "watch" or "listen" pill on every Lecture entry points to one of these five free, complete courses — not a clip or a summary, the actual class.

Justice

Taught by Michael Sandel, Anne T. and Robert M. Bass Professor of Government at Harvard. One of the most-watched university courses ever recorded, chosen for "The Moral Side of Murder" because Sandel is the person who made this exact case study famous.

Used on Power, Justice & State
Philosophy and the Science of Human Nature

Taught by Tamar Gendler, Professor of Philosophy and Psychology at Yale, as part of Open Yale Courses — full recorded lectures, free, no login. Chosen because it pairs philosophical texts with the psychology behind why we resist them.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Ring of Gyges), Individual & Collective (Hobbes)
Death

Taught by Shelly Kagan, Clark Professor of Philosophy at Yale, also via Open Yale Courses. One of Yale's most popular electives, chosen because Kagan argues the material rigorously rather than just surveying what other philosophers have said.

Used on Mortality, Fate & Transcendence
European Civilization, 1648–1945

Taught by John Merriman, Charles Seymour Professor of History at Yale, via Open Yale Courses. Chosen for its "Industrial Revolutions" lecture because Merriman ties the history directly to the art and anxieties it produced.

Used on Hubris, Progress & Nature
Human Behavioral Biology

Taught by Robert Sapolsky, Professor of Biology and Neurology at Stanford. A full-semester course posted free by Stanford, chosen because Sapolsky covers the biological roots of behavior with a rare mix of rigor and humor.

Used on Cosmic Awe & Evolution (Behavioral Evolution), The Stranger, The Self & The Other (Aggression, III)
Go deeper

Podcasts & video analysis

The "deeper" pill on a work points to one of these. Every host below has a real, checkable credential in the subject — a professorship, a curatorship, or a career built on it — not just a strong opinion.

History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps

Hosted by Peter Adamson, Professor of Philosophy at LMU Munich and King's College London. A long-running, chapter-by-chapter survey of the entire history of philosophy, chosen because Adamson is a working academic explaining primary texts directly, not summarizing secondhand.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Plato's Cave), Individual & Collective (Marcus Aurelius), Power, Justice & State (Machiavelli)
The ThinkND Podcast

Notre Dame's own public scholarship platform. The Rear Window episode features Barry McCrea, a Notre Dame professor of literature, chosen for a genuine close reading of the film rather than a general recap.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Rear Window)
The Lonely Palette

Hosted by Tamar Avishai, an art historian (Tufts, former Harvard teaching fellow) who dedicates a full episode to one painting at a time. Chosen over general survey podcasts because it goes deep on a single work instead of skimming many.

Used on Individual & Collective (Seurat, La Grande Jatte)
Bloomsbury Academic Podcast

Interviews the actual authors of Bloomsbury's academic film-studies books. The Godfather episodes feature Jon Lewis, University Distinguished Professor of Film Studies at Oregon State and author of the BFI Film Classics volume on this exact film — about as direct a source as a podcast can be.

Used on Power, Justice & State (The Godfather)
The Great Books (National Review)

A different named scholar discusses each novel: Jessica Hooten Wilson (University of Dallas) on Crime and Punishment, Amy Sturgis on Frankenstein, Dwight Lindley (Hillsdale College) on Middlemarch. Chosen because it's built for exactly this use case: an accessible way into a long novel without having to read it first.

Used on Mortality, Fate & Transcendence, Hubris, Progress & Nature, The Stranger, The Self & The Other
Historic Royal Palaces Podcast

The official podcast of the UK charity that runs Hampton Court Palace and the Tower of London. Chosen because Holbein's The Ambassadors hangs in the National Gallery, but its Tudor court context is exactly what this institution specializes in.

Used on Mortality, Fate & Transcendence (Holbein, The Ambassadors)
ArtCurious

Hosted by Jennifer Dasal, a curator at the North Carolina Museum of Art. The "Shock Art" episode covering Manet's Olympia was chosen for its focus on exactly why the painting scandalized its first audience, not just a biography of the painter.

Used on The Stranger, The Self & The Other (Manet, Olympia)
Painting of the Week

Hosted by Phil Grabsky, a BAFTA-winning documentarian behind the Exhibition on Screen film series. Chosen for Turner's Rain, Steam and Speed because each episode is a focused, single-painting close read rather than a general art-history survey.

Used on Hubris, Progress & Nature (Turner, Rain, Steam and Speed)
Smarthistory (video)

Founded and narrated by Dr. Beth Harris and Dr. Steven Zucker, art historians who built Smarthistory as an open, museum-partnered alternative to a paywalled textbook. Used as an embedded video rather than a text link on works where they'd made a video, since hearing two historians actually look at the painting beats a written summary.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Magritte), Power, Justice & State (David, The Death of Socrates)
NPR Fresh Air

NPR's long-running interview and culture program, chosen where it had an episode that placed a specific work in its real political or cultural context — Fela Kuti's music and the Nigerian state, or an album's reception on release — better than a standard studio-recording review could.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Daft Punk review), Power, Justice & State (Fela Kuti, Zombie)
Dissect

A single podcast season is devoted to one album, track by track. Chosen for Kendrick Lamar's To Pimp a Butterfly because a 20-plus episode close reading of a concept album is a level of depth nothing else offered matched.

Used on The Stranger, The Self & The Other (Kendrick Lamar, To Pimp a Butterfly)
Quick take

Reviews & criticism

The "review" pill points here — a short, credentialed take on whether a work holds up, for when you want a few minutes of context, not a deep dive.

RogerEbert.com

Roger Ebert's own reviews where he wrote one, and his successor critics (Matt Zoller Seitz, Brian Tallerico, and others) where he didn't. Chosen because it's the most consistently well-written, opinionated, and honest film criticism freely available online, on almost every film on this site.

Used across every film entry, all seven themes
Smarthistory (text)

The same Beth Harris / Steven Zucker team as above, in short written form for artworks that don't have (or didn't need) a full embedded video review.

Used across most art entries
Rolling Stone

Chosen for Pink Floyd's The Dark Side of the Moon as the outlet with the deepest institutional history of writing about this specific record.

Used on Individual & Collective (Pink Floyd)
Encyclopaedia Britannica

Used where a short, reliably fact-checked reference entry was the right amount of context — classical music and a handful of paintings without a dedicated critical writeup elsewhere.

Used on several music and art entries, Cosmic Awe & Evolution through Hubris, Progress & Nature
The work itself

Museums & primary sources

The "view" pill always points to the institution that actually owns and studies the piece — the closest thing to a primary source a website link can offer.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art, LACMA, the Art Institute of Chicago, the National Gallery (London), and the Musée d'Orsay each hold one of the paintings covered here, and each entry links directly to that museum's own collection page for it.
