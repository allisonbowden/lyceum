The lecture itself

University lecture courses

The "watch" or "listen" pill on every Lecture entry points to one of these five courses. Full recordings, free, no login — the actual class, not a clip.

Justice

Michael Sandel teaches this at Harvard, and "The Moral Side of Murder" is the lecture that made his trolley-problem case study famous in the first place. It's one of the most-watched courses ever put online.

Used on Power, Justice & State
Philosophy and the Science of Human Nature

Tamar Gendler, Yale, via Open Yale Courses. She puts philosophical texts next to the psychology of why we resist them — not just what Plato or Hobbes argued, but why the argument still gets under your skin.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Ring of Gyges), Individual & Collective (Hobbes)
Death

Also Open Yale Courses, taught by Shelly Kagan — one of Yale's most popular electives for a reason. Kagan builds the argument step by step instead of just cataloguing what other philosophers have said about it.

Used on Mortality, Fate & Transcendence
European Civilization, 1648–1945

John Merriman, Yale. His "Industrial Revolutions" lecture is the rare history lecture that actually connects the events to the art and anxiety they produced, not just the dates.

Used on Hubris, Progress & Nature
Human Behavioral Biology

Stanford posted Robert Sapolsky's entire course free on YouTube. He's funny in a way biology lectures rarely are, and it doesn't cost him any rigor.

Used on Cosmic Awe & Evolution (Behavioral Evolution), The Stranger, The Self & The Other (Aggression, III)
Go deeper

Podcasts & video analysis

The "deeper" pill on a work points to one of these. All the hosts have a real credential behind them — a professorship, a curatorship, a career spent on this material.

History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps

Peter Adamson (LMU Munich, King's College London) works through the history of philosophy chapter by chapter, straight from the primary texts. No secondhand summarizing.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Plato's Cave), Individual & Collective (Marcus Aurelius), Power, Justice & State (Machiavelli)
The ThinkND Podcast

Notre Dame's public scholarship platform. Barry McCrea, a literature professor there, does a proper close reading of Rear Window — not a plot recap.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Rear Window)
The Lonely Palette

Tamar Avishai (art historian, Tufts) spends a full episode on a single painting. For Seurat's La Grande Jatte, that's exactly the pace it needs.

Used on Individual & Collective (Seurat, La Grande Jatte)
Bloomsbury Academic Podcast

The Godfather episode is Jon Lewis, who literally wrote the BFI Film Classics book on this movie. Hard to get closer to the source than the guy who wrote the book.

Used on Power, Justice & State (The Godfather)
The Great Books (National Review)

A different scholar per novel — Jessica Hooten Wilson (University of Dallas) on Crime and Punishment, Amy Sturgis on Frankenstein, Dwight Lindley (Hillsdale) on Middlemarch. An easy way into a long novel you haven't read yet.

Used on Mortality, Fate & Transcendence, Hubris, Progress & Nature, The Stranger, The Self & The Other
Historic Royal Palaces Podcast

Run by the charity that manages Hampton Court and the Tower of London. Holbein's The Ambassadors hangs in the National Gallery, but the Tudor court intrigue behind it is their exact territory.

Used on Mortality, Fate & Transcendence (Holbein, The Ambassadors)
ArtCurious

Jennifer Dasal, a curator at the North Carolina Museum of Art, has a "Shock Art" episode on Manet's Olympia that's really about why it scandalized Paris — not a Manet biography with the painting mentioned in passing.

Used on The Stranger, The Self & The Other (Manet, Olympia)
Painting of the Week

Phil Grabsky, the BAFTA-winning documentarian behind Exhibition on Screen, gives Turner's Rain, Steam and Speed its own focused episode.

Used on Hubris, Progress & Nature (Turner, Rain, Steam and Speed)
Smarthistory (video)

Dr. Beth Harris and Dr. Steven Zucker built Smarthistory as a free, museum-backed alternative to an art history textbook. Where they'd filmed a video on the piece, that's what's embedded — two historians actually looking at the painting together beats reading about it.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Magritte), Power, Justice & State (David, The Death of Socrates)
NPR Fresh Air

Used where Fresh Air had actually put a work in its real context — Fela Kuti's music against the Nigerian state, or how an album landed when it first came out.

Used on Truth, Illusion & Deception (Daft Punk review), Power, Justice & State (Fela Kuti, Zombie)
Dissect

One album, one season, track by track. Nothing else covers Kendrick Lamar's To Pimp a Butterfly at anywhere near this depth.

Used on The Stranger, The Self & The Other (Kendrick Lamar, To Pimp a Butterfly)
Quick take

Reviews & criticism

The "review" pill points here — a short, credentialed take on whether a work holds up, for when you want a few minutes of context, not a deep dive.

RogerEbert.com

Ebert's own review where he wrote one; Matt Zoller Seitz, Brian Tallerico, or another of his successors where he didn't. Still the best free film criticism on the web, film after film.

Used across every film entry, all seven themes
Smarthistory (text)

Same Harris/Zucker team as above, just the written version, for the paintings that didn't get a video.

Used across most art entries
Rolling Stone

Nobody's written about Pink Floyd's The Dark Side of the Moon longer or better.

Used on Individual & Collective (Pink Floyd)
Encyclopaedia Britannica

For the handful of pieces — mostly classical music, a few paintings — that didn't have a real critical writeup anywhere else. A solid, fact-checked entry beats nothing.

Used on several music and art entries, Cosmic Awe & Evolution through Hubris, Progress & Nature
